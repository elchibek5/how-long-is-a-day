How long is a day?

On Earth, 24 hours. On Jupiter, less than 10. On Venus, 117 Earth days, and the Sun rises in the west. I wanted to know why. The answer is hiding in a few circles, one small angle and a tilt.

01

Notice + Name

A day is not the same everywhere

What I noticed

Every day of my life has been 24 hours long, so I never questioned it. Then I saw this photo. It is a real sunset on Mars, taken by NASA's Curiosity rover. Hills, a setting Sun, the end of a day. It looks almost like home, except the sky glows blue.

It made me ask a simple question: if Mars has sunsets, how long is a day on Mars? My first guess was that Mars gets maybe 4 hours of daylight. I was completely wrong, and the more planets I looked up, the stranger the answers became.

Why it caught my attention

A "day" sounds like a fact you memorize. But it is actually a geometric event: a point on a spinning ball turning until it faces the Sun again. Change the spin, the orbit or the tilt and you change the day.

My first guesses vs. what is actually true

I wrote these down before doing any math. Every one of them was wrong, and each mistake pointed me to a better question.

My guess

"Mars probably has about 4 hours of daylight."

What's true

A Mars day (called a sol) is 24 h 39 m 35 s. At the equator the Sun is up for about 12 h 20 m, almost the same as Earth.

My guess

"Bigger planets spin slower, so their days are longer."

What's true

Jupiter, the biggest planet, has the shortest day: 9 h 56 m. Size does not decide the day.

My guess

"A day is just one spin of the planet."

What's true

Earth spins once in 23 h 56 m 4 s, but a day is 24 h. One spin is not one day. Why? That became my main question.

My guess

"A day is always much shorter than a year."

What's true

On Mercury one day lasts two of its years. On Venus one spin takes longer than its year.

Naming the geometry

A day turned out to be a story about three angles and one plane.

Spin angle

How far the planet has turned on its axis. One full spin = 360°.

Orbit angle θ

How far the planet has moved around the Sun. One full orbit (a year) = 360°.

Tilt angle

The angle between the spin axis and "straight up" from the orbit. Earth: 23.4°. Uranus: 97.8°.

The day–night plane

Sunlight lights exactly half of a ball. The edge, called the terminator, is a great circle through the center.

The questions I investigated

  1. Why is a day on Earth 24 hours if Earth spins once in 23 h 56 m?
  2. Is there one rule that explains Earth's 24 hours, Mercury's 176 days and Venus's backwards Sun?
  3. How long is the Sun actually up, and why does that change with where you stand and the season?

02

Explain the mathematics

A day is an angle

2.1 Turning time into angles

A spinning planet is a rotating circle. If one full turn of 360° takes a time T, then every hour the planet turns 360° ÷ T. This is called angular speed.

That 15° is real geography: time zones are about 15° of longitude wide, because the Sun appears to move 15° across the sky every hour. Our clocks are secretly protractors.

The same formula works on every world. Jupiter turns 36.3° every hour. Venus turns only 0.06° per hour, and backwards.

2.2 Why one spin is not one day

Follow a person standing on a planet at noon. Scroll slowly: the diagram moves with you.

Start at noon

Our person is standing where the Sun is straight overhead; the blue line points from the planet's center through them, right at the Sun. The planet is also moving along its orbit.

One full spin: 360°

The planet turns all the way around once. On Earth this takes 23 h 56 m 4 s, called a sidereal day ("star day"), because the person now points at the same distant star as before. But the planet has moved along its orbit, so the Sun is not overhead yet.

The missing angle θ

The planet moved through an angle θ around the Sun. The person's line is parallel to where it started (both point at the same far-away star). The line to the Sun cuts across these two parallel lines, so the two angles marked θ are alternate angles, and alternate angles are equal. The person is exactly θ short of noon.

Turn a little more

After turning the extra θ, the Sun is overhead again. So one solar day, the day our clocks measure, is a spin of 360° + θ.

Earth's real numbers

Earth goes 360° around the Sun in 365.26 days, so θ ≈ 360° ÷ 365.26 ≈ 0.986° per day. Turning that extra 0.986° takes about 3 min 56 s. And 23 h 56 m 4 s + 3 m 56 s = 24 h. The mystery is solved by an angle smaller than one degree.

What if a planet spins backwards?

Venus spins the opposite way to its orbit. Now the orbit helps: the Sun comes back after a turn of only 360° − θ. That is why a Venus day (117 Earth days) is shorter than one Venus spin (243 Earth days).

2.4 How long is the Sun up?

Sunlight arrives in parallel rays, so it lights exactly half of a ball. The boundary between day and night is a great circle: a flat plane through the planet's center, perpendicular to the sunlight.

As the planet spins, you ride around on your circle of latitude. The fraction of that circle that is in sunlight is the fraction of the day that you get daylight.

No tilt: the day–night plane passes through both poles and cuts every latitude circle exactly in half. Everyone gets 50% daylight, every day of the year. With tilt: as the planet orbits, the Sun is overhead at a different latitude δ (the Sun's declination), the plane cuts the circles unevenly, and we get seasons.

Working out where the plane cuts your circle (with a little trigonometry) gives the half-arc of daylight H0:

φ is your latitude and T is the solar day from 2.3. If tan φ · tan δ goes above 1 or below −1, the equation has no solution (a cosine can't be bigger than 1), and that is exactly what happens in nature: there is no sunrise or sunset at all. The Sun circles the sky all day (midnight sun) or never rises (polar night). This happens beyond latitude 90° − tilt, the polar circles.

Checking my model against reality

For San Francisco (37.8° N) on the June solstice my formula gives 14 h 37 m of daylight. The real sunrise-to-sunset time is about 14 h 47 m. My model is 10 minutes short, because Earth's air bends sunlight over the horizon and the Sun is a disk, not a point. Still within about 1%.

Uranus breaks the pattern

Uranus is tilted 97.8°, so its polar circles sit just 7.8° from the equator. Almost the whole planet gets a midnight sun at some point. Near the poles, the Sun stays up for about 42 Earth years, then disappears for 42 years, on a planet that spins once every 17 hours.

2.6 Why this matters

Living on Mars time

When NASA landed the Spirit and Opportunity rovers in 2004, the team lived on Mars time, starting work about 40 minutes later every day, wearing watches adjusted to run on Mars time. Future astronauts will need clocks and calendars built on a 24 h 39 m 35 s sol, about 668.6 sols per Martian year.

Solar power & sustainability

The daylight formula tells engineers how many hours of sunshine a solar panel gets at any latitude and season, on Earth for solar farms, and on Mars for rovers that plan each day around their sunlight.

Life above the Arctic Circle

In Utqiaġvik, Alaska (71.3° N) the Sun does not rise for about 65 days each winter. My pure-geometry model predicts 73 days; the air bending sunlight explains the difference. Communities plan energy, health and food around these numbers.

Telescopes & satellites

Stars return 3 m 56 s earlier every night, because they follow the 23 h 56 m spin, not the 24 h solar day. Observatories and satellite operators keep "sidereal time" for exactly this reason.

Worlds with endless days

Many planets around small red stars probably spin exactly once per orbit. Then 1/Tspin − 1/Tyear = 0, and the day is infinitely long: one side always day, one side always night. Scientists wonder if life could exist in the twilight ring between them.

Create

Sunlight Mandalas

I turned the daylight formula into art. Each mandala is one planet's whole year of sunlight in a single circle, every latitude and every season at once. Every pixel is calculated with cos H0 = −tan φ · tan δ.

  • Angle = season. Start at the top (spring) and go clockwise through one full orbit: 360° = one year.
  • Distance from the center = latitude. South pole in the middle, equator in the middle ring, north pole on the edge.
  • Color = how much of the day the Sun is up, from deep night (0%) through rose (exactly half) to warm white (the Sun never sets).

What my own art showed me: Earth, Mars, Saturn and Neptune draw almost the same mandala, because their tilts are almost the same (23°–28°). Distance from the Sun, size and day length do not change the shape at all. Only the tilt does.

The choices I made, and the math behind them

1

A circle for the year

Seasons repeat, so I drew the year as a circle: the angle around the mandala is the planet's angle around the Sun. One orbit = 360°.

2

Fractions, not hours

I colored by the fraction of the day, not hours. That way a 10-hour Jupiter day and a 24-hour Earth day are compared fairly, by shape alone.

3

Rose means "exactly half"

The equator ring and the two equinox spokes are always rose (50% daylight) on every planet. It's a built-in check: if they weren't, my math would be wrong.

4

The yin-yang symmetry

Half a year later, every place gets the opposite: 70% daylight in June means 30% in December. Algebraically, δ becomes −δ, so cos H₀ changes sign. That makes each mandala look like a yin-yang.

5

Contour lines by solving backwards

The thin lines mark exactly 25%, 50% and 75% daylight. I found them by solving the formula for the latitude: φ = arctan(−cos(180°·f) ÷ tan δ).

04

Mathematical mindset

What changed in my thinking

My mistakes moved me forward

My "4 hours on Mars" guess was wrong, but checking it made me notice that Earth's spin (23 h 56 m) and Earth's day (24 h) are different. That small gap became the center of the whole project. If I had been right at the start, I would never have found the 360° + θ idea.

Assumptions I had to question

  • "The Sun is a point and there is no air." Real daylight in San Francisco is 10 minutes longer than my model, and Utqiaġvik's polar night is 8 days shorter. The atmosphere bends light.
  • "Orbits are circles." Mercury's orbit is an ellipse. Near the Sun it moves so fast that, from some places, the Sun rises, stops, goes backwards and rises again. My circle model gets the average day right but misses this wobble.
  • "The season doesn't change during one day." True on Earth (a day is 1/365 of a year), false on Mercury, where a day is two years. So I treat my daylight numbers for Mercury and Venus as approximations.
  • "Numbers in tables are final." Saturn's day was corrected in 2019 using waves in its rings, and Uranus's in 2025 using Hubble. Many tables still print the old values. Even scientists keep revising.

How my thinking grew

I started with a list of strange facts and ended with one formula that explains all of them. A day is not a number you memorize; it is what happens when two rotations race each other, plus a tilt that decides who gets the light.

The hardest part was step 2.2: I could not see why the extra angle had to be exactly θ until I drew the two lines pointing at the same distant star and recognized parallel lines cut by a transversal, something from basic geometry hiding in the solar system.

What I still wonder

  • Could a moon of Jupiter have a "day" controlled by Jupiter instead of the Sun?
  • If people lived on Mars, would their bodies adjust to 24 h 39 m days?
  • Is there a planet somewhere with a day of exactly one year, and what is the weather like on the line between its day and night sides?
